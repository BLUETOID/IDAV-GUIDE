1. Regression Models

Use regression to predict a numerical outcome or quantify conditional associations; logistic regression instead predicts a class probability.

Practice: Q17 · definition and uses · Q22 · model types · Q27 · multiple predictors.

Definition

Regression analysis is a supervised statistical technique that models the mathematical relationship between a dependent variable (the outcome to predict, Y) and one or more independent variables (the predictors, X). The model equation is used for prediction on new inputs and for describing associations between predictors and the outcome; coefficients alone do not establish causation.

Types of Regression Models

1.1 Simple Linear Regression

Models the linear relationship between one predictor and the outcome:

Y = b0 + b1X + ε

Where b0 is the intercept (value of Y when X = 0), b1 is the slope (change in Y per unit increase in X), and ε is the error term (unexplained variation). The coefficients b0 and b1 are estimated by Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), which minimizes the sum of squared residuals: minimize Σ(Yi − Ŷi)².

1.2 Multiple Linear Regression

Y = b0 + b1X1 + b2X2 + ... + bnXn + ε

Each coefficient bi represents the predicted change in Y for a unit increase in Xi, holding the other included predictors constant. In observational data, omitted confounders can still bias this association; a causal interpretation requires additional assumptions or a suitable study design.

1.3 Logistic Regression

Used for binary classification (outcome is 0 or 1). Models the log-odds of the positive class: log(p / 1−p) = b0 + b1X1 + ... The log-odds is passed through the sigmoid function σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z) to produce a probability in (0,1). Despite the name "regression," logistic regression is a classification algorithm.

1.4 Polynomial Regression

Fits a polynomial curve: Y = b0 + b1X + b2X² + ... + bnXn. Handles non-linear relationships between X and Y while remaining linear in the coefficients — solvable with OLS after creating polynomial feature columns.

1.5 Ridge Regression (L2 Regularization)

Adds a penalty λΣbi² to the OLS loss function. Shrinks all coefficients toward zero (but not exactly zero), reducing the impact of multicollinear predictors. The regularization strength λ is a hyperparameter: larger λ → more shrinkage → simpler model.

1.6 Lasso Regression (L1 Regularization)

Adds a penalty λΣ|bi| to the OLS loss function. Unlike Ridge, Lasso can shrink coefficients to exactly zero — performing automatic feature selection. Preferred when you have many predictors and want a sparse model.

OLS Derivation Concept

OLS finds the coefficients that minimize the Residual Sum of Squares (RSS): RSS = Σ(Yi − b0 − b1Xi)². Taking partial derivatives with respect to b0 and b1 and setting to zero yields the normal equations. Solution: b1 = Σ(Xi − X̄)(Yi − Ȳ) / Σ(Xi − X̄)², and b0 = Ȳ − b1X̄. In matrix notation, if X includes an intercept and XᵀX is invertible: β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹XᵀY (otherwise use a pseudoinverse or regularization).

R-Squared (Coefficient of Determination)

R² = 1 − (RSS / TSS), where TSS = Σ(Yi − Ȳ)² is the total variance in Y. For an OLS fit evaluated on its training data with an intercept, R² measures the fraction of sample variation around the mean accounted for by the fit: 0 is the mean baseline and 1 is a perfect fit. Outside this setting (for example on held-out data), R² can be negative. Adjusted R² penalizes extra predictors but does not by itself prevent overfitting; check held-out performance.

OLS Assumptions and Diagnostics

For the usual OLS interpretation and inference, examine the following. Linearity and zero conditional mean of errors, together with appropriate independence and constant error variance, underpin the classical best-linear-unbiased (Gauss–Markov) result; normal errors are mainly useful for small-sample tests, not for unbiasedness:

  • Linear conditional mean and exogeneity: Model the conditional mean linearly in the coefficients, with errors averaging to zero given the included predictors. Inspect residual patterns; omitted confounders and reverse causality can violate exogeneity.
  • Error dependence: Check whether errors are correlated over time; the Durbin–Watson statistic can help detect first-order autocorrelation. If present, ordinary standard errors may be unreliable without adjustment.
  • Normality for classical small-sample inference: A Q–Q plot helps assess error distribution. Normality is not required for OLS to estimate coefficients; large-sample inference needs its own conditions.
  • Equal Variance (Homoscedasticity): The variance of ε is constant across all X values. Violated when residuals fan out as fitted values increase (heteroscedasticity). Detected via Breusch-Pagan test.
  • No perfect multicollinearity: Predictors must not be exact linear combinations of one another. Strong near-collinearity makes individual coefficients unstable; inspect VIF in context and consider Ridge regression or removing redundant predictors.

Figure 1: Linear regression scatter plot. Red/maroon points are observed data; the maroon line is the fitted OLS regression line Ŷ = b₀ + b₁X. The dashed vertical segment illustrates a residual ε — the vertical distance between an observed point and the predicted value on the line. OLS minimizes the sum of all such squared residuals.

2. Multivariate Analysis

Study variables jointly instead of one at a time: correlations, multiple predictors and latent factors reveal different kinds of structure.

Practice: Q9 · why it matters · Q32 · univariate vs multivariate · Q33 · mixed-method design.

Definition

Multivariate analysis is a collection of statistical methods for simultaneously analyzing observations on two or more variables, studying their joint distribution, relationships, and interactions. It captures patterns and structures that are invisible when variables are examined one at a time.

Correlation Matrix

A correlation matrix shows the pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients between all pairs of variables. Each entry rij ∈ [−1, +1]: r = +1 means perfect positive linear relationship; r = 0 means no linear relationship; r = −1 means perfect negative relationship. The strength of a correlation depends on the domain and measurement context; these coefficients do not measure nonlinear dependence. The correlation matrix is the starting point for PCA, factor analysis, and multicollinearity detection in regression.

3. Bayesian Modeling and Bayesian Networks

Bayesian inference updates a prior with evidence; a Bayesian network organizes the joint distribution using a directed acyclic graph.

Practice: Q16 · network components · Q25 · Bayesian modeling.

Bayes' Theorem

P(H | D) = [ P(D | H) × P(H) ] / P(D)

  • Prior P(H): Belief about hypothesis H before observing data D.
  • Likelihood P(D|H): Probability of observing data D if H is true.
  • Posterior P(H|D): Updated belief about H after observing D — the key output.
  • Marginal likelihood P(D): Normalizing constant; ensures posterior sums to 1 over all hypotheses.

The fundamental Bayesian insight: prior beliefs + observed evidence = updated posterior belief. How strongly evidence changes the prior depends on the likelihood, sample size and model assumptions.

Naive Bayes Classifier

Naive Bayes applies Bayes' theorem to classification, with the "naive" assumption that all features are conditionally independent given the class label. Despite this unrealistic assumption, Naive Bayes works surprisingly well in practice, especially for text classification:

P(class | features) ∝ P(class) × Π P(featurei | class)

Example — Email Spam Detection: You want to classify an email containing the words "free," "money," and "click" as spam or not-spam.

  • P(spam) = 0.3 (30% of emails are spam — prior)
  • P(free | spam) = 0.8, P(money | spam) = 0.7, P(click | spam) = 0.75
  • P(free | not-spam) = 0.1, P(money | not-spam) = 0.1, P(click | not-spam) = 0.05
  • P(spam | email) ∝ 0.3 × 0.8 × 0.7 × 0.75 = 0.126
  • P(not-spam | email) ∝ 0.7 × 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.05 = 0.00035
  • After normalization: P(spam | these words) ≈ 0.997 under this illustrative model — classified as spam.

Bayesian Network — Structure

A Bayesian Network (BN) is a probabilistic graphical model represented as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where:

  • Nodes represent random variables (discrete or continuous).
  • Directed edges encode conditional dependencies: A → B means A is a parent used in B's conditional distribution. A causal reading needs extra assumptions, not just a DAG.
  • Conditional Probability Table (CPT): Each node X with parents Pa(X) has a table giving P(X | Pa(X)) for each combination of parent values. Root nodes (no parents) have unconditional prior probabilities.
  • Joint distribution factorization: P(X1, ..., Xn) = Π P(Xi | Pa(Xi)). This factorization is valid because the DAG encodes conditional independence: each node is conditionally independent of its non-descendants given its parents.

Figure 2: Bayesian Network DAG for medical diagnosis. Nodes are Smoking, Air Pollution (root nodes — prior probabilities only), Cancer (has a CPT conditioned on both parents), X-Ray Result, and Dyspnoea (both conditioned on Cancer). Given observed evidence (e.g., patient smokes + has dyspnoea), inference computes P(Cancer | evidence) by propagating through the graph.

Inference in Bayesian Networks

Exact inference (Variable Elimination, Junction Tree) computes exact posterior probabilities but is exponential in the treewidth of the network. Approximate inference (Markov Chain Monte Carlo — MCMC, Loopy Belief Propagation) scales better but produces estimates with sampling error. Feasibility depends more on graph structure and variable cardinality than on node count alone.

4. Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Kernel Methods

First understand the linear maximum-margin boundary; then see how kernels produce nonlinear boundaries in input space.

Practice: Q14 · nonlinear classification · Q29 · kernel functions · Q39 · SVM and kernels.

Definition

SVM is a supervised learning algorithm that finds the maximum-margin hyperplane — the decision boundary that separates two classes with the largest possible margin. The margin is defined as the minimum distance from the hyperplane to the nearest training points of either class (the support vectors). Margin maximization is one way to control model complexity, but generalization also depends on the kernel, regularization and data.

Mathematical Formulation

Find weight vector w and bias b such that:

  • Decision boundary: w · x + b = 0
  • Support vector planes: w · x + b = +1 (positive class support) and −1 (negative class support)
  • Objective: minimize (1/2)||w||² subject to yi(w · xi + b) ≥ 1 for all i, where yi ∈ {+1, −1}

Hard Margin vs Soft Margin SVM

Hard margin SVM requires all training points to be correctly classified with margin ≥ 1. This fails when data is not linearly separable (any two overlapping classes in real-world data). Soft margin SVM introduces slack variables ξi ≥ 0 that allow misclassifications, each at a cost:

Minimize: (1/2)||w||² + C × Σξi subject to yi(w · xi + b) ≥ 1 − ξi

The hyperparameter C controls the bias-variance tradeoff: large C → low bias / high variance (tries to classify all training points correctly, even at the cost of small margin); small C → high bias / low variance (allows more misclassifications but achieves a wider margin).

The Kernel Trick

When data is not linearly separable in the original feature space, the kernel trick implicitly maps data into a higher-dimensional space where a linear boundary can represent a nonlinear boundary in the original inputs (not necessarily perfect separation). The SVM optimization problem is reformulated in terms of dot products xi · xj (the dual formulation). Replacing each dot product with a kernel function K(xi, xj) = φ(xi) · φ(xj) allows the algorithm to operate in the high-dimensional (possibly infinite-dimensional) feature space without explicitly computing the mapping φ.

Multi-Class SVM Strategies

  • One-vs-Rest (OvR): Train k binary SVMs, one per class vs all others. Classify a new point by the SVM with the highest confidence score. Simple, requires k classifiers.
  • One-vs-One (OvO): Train k(k-1)/2 binary SVMs, one for each pair of classes. Classify by majority vote. More classifiers but each trains on a smaller, balanced dataset. Accuracy depends on data and tuning; compare strategies on validation data.

5. Time Series Analysis

Time order matters: identify trend and seasonality, then distinguish linear dependence on past values from state-dependent nonlinear dynamics.

Practice: Q12 · time-dependent data · Q26 · linear vs nonlinear · Q33 · method selection.

Definition

A time series is a sequence of observations Xt ordered in time (t = 1, 2, ..., T); the models below generally assume regular intervals, so irregular observations may need special treatment. Time series analysis models the temporal structure of the data — autocorrelation, trend, seasonality — to describe, forecast, and detect anomalies.

Decomposition of a Time Series

A useful descriptive decomposition separates a series into components when these patterns are present; not every series has all four or a unique decomposition:

  • Trend (T): The long-term, smooth direction of change over time — growth, decline, or flatness. Estimated by moving averages or polynomial regression on time.
  • Seasonality (S): Regular, periodic fluctuations with a fixed period (daily, weekly, monthly, yearly). Example: retail sales spike every December; electricity demand peaks every weekday morning.
  • Cyclical variation (C): Irregular, long-period oscillations not tied to a calendar (e.g., 5–10 year economic cycles). Unlike seasonality, period and amplitude are variable.
  • Residual / Irregular (R): The random noise remaining after removing T, S, and C. Ideally resembles white noise after an adequate model, though this must be checked rather than assumed.

Additive model: Xt = Tt + St + Ct + Rt — used when seasonal amplitude is constant. Multiplicative model: Xt = Tt × St × Ct × Rt — used when seasonal amplitude grows proportionally with the trend level.

Stationarity

A weakly stationary series has a constant mean and variance and autocovariance that depends on lag, not calendar time. ARMA models typically assume stationarity; ARIMA applies differencing, ΔXt = Xt − Xt−1, to address some kinds of non-stationarity. The Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test has a unit-root null; rejecting it provides evidence against a unit root, not proof that every stationary-model assumption holds.

Autocorrelation and Partial Autocorrelation

Autocorrelation Function (ACF): ACF(k) = correlation between Xt and Xt−k. Measures how much the series correlates with itself at lag k. For an ideal stationary pure MA(q) process, the ACF cuts off after lag q (a useful diagnostic, not a guarantee). Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF): PACF(k) = correlation between Xt and Xt−k after removing the linear influence of intermediate lags 1, ..., k−1. For an ideal stationary pure AR(p) process, the PACF cuts off after lag p.

Linear Time Series Models

5.1 AR(p) — Autoregressive Model

Xt = c + φ1Xt−1 + φ2Xt−2 + ... + φpXt−p + εt

The current value is a linear combination of the last p values plus white noise. Select order p using the PACF: PACF cuts off after lag p for a pure AR(p) process.

5.2 MA(q) — Moving Average Model

Xt = μ + εt + θ1εt−1 + ... + θqεt−q

The current value depends on the current and past error (shock) terms. Select order q using the ACF: ACF cuts off after lag q for a pure MA(q) process.

5.3 ARIMA(p, d, q)

Combines AR(p), d-order differencing (for stationarity), and MA(q). The Box-Jenkins methodology guides model selection:

  1. Identification: Plot the series; check stationarity with ADF test; apply differencing (d times) until stationary; plot ACF and PACF to select p and q.
  2. Estimation: Estimate parameters, often by likelihood-based methods (implementation depends on model).
  3. Diagnostic checking: Examine residuals — should be white noise (no autocorrelation, verified by Ljung-Box test). If residuals show patterns, revise model.
  4. Forecasting: Use the validated model to forecast h steps ahead, with prediction intervals that widen with the forecast horizon.

Model selection criterion: AIC (Akaike Information Criterion) = 2k − 2ln(L) and BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion) = k·ln(n) − 2ln(L), where k = number of parameters, L = likelihood. When comparing models on the same data, lower is preferred; BIC usually penalizes extra parameters more strongly than AIC for sufficiently large n.

5.4 SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)[m] — Seasonal ARIMA

Extends ARIMA with seasonal AR and MA components at lag m (the season length: m=12 for monthly data, m=4 for quarterly, m=7 for daily with weekly seasonality). SARIMA handles both non-seasonal and seasonal patterns in a single unified model.

Nonlinear Time Series Models

ARCH / GARCH — Volatility Modeling

ARCH(q) (Autoregressive Conditional Heteroscedasticity): models the variance of the error term as a function of past squared errors: σt² = α0 + α1εt−1² + ... + αqεt−q². This captures volatility clustering — periods of high volatility tend to follow each other. GARCH(p,q) additionally includes past variances: σt² = α0 + Σαiεt−i² + Σβjσt−j². GARCH is commonly used to model financial-return volatility and can inform risk estimates; results depend on model fit and distributional assumptions.

LSTM — Long Short-Term Memory Networks

LSTMs are recurrent neural networks with a specialized memory cell that selectively stores, updates, and forgets information over many time steps via three gates: the forget gate (decides what information to discard), the input gate (decides what new information to store), and the output gate (decides what to output). LSTMs can learn nonlinear temporal dependencies, though long-range learning is not guaranteed. Compare them against seasonal or linear baselines using time-ordered validation; model choice depends on the data, not a universal performance ranking.

Linear Systems Analysis vs Nonlinear Dynamics

A linear system combines past observations or shocks with fixed coefficients: for example, Xt = 0.7Xt−1 + εt is an AR(1) model. A one-unit change in the previous value changes the conditional prediction by 0.7 units at every level. ARMA models combine such lagged terms and shocks; check residual autocorrelation and forecast error on later time periods.

Nonlinear dynamics use a state-dependent update. For example, the illustrative logistic map Xt+1 = rXt(1 − Xt) (for states between 0 and 1) has a quadratic feedback term. Its response to the same change in Xt depends on the current state; depending on r, it can settle, oscillate or behave chaotically. Chaos means sensitivity to initial conditions in a deterministic model, not simply random noise. GARCH instead models nonlinear conditional variance, while LSTMs learn nonlinear update functions from training data; these are different notions of nonlinearity.

Worked choice: For monthly sales, first plot trend and seasonality, fit a seasonal linear baseline on earlier months and inspect later-month errors. If residuals retain systematic state-dependent patterns, test an appropriate nonlinear model on the same held-out period. A curving plot by itself is not proof of chaos.

6. Rule Induction

Turn data into readable IF–THEN conditions; check both how often a rule applies and how often its conclusion is correct.

Practice: Q13 · rule example · Q30 · concepts and types.

Definition

Rule induction is a supervised machine learning technique that automatically extracts a set of IF-THEN (antecedent → consequent) rules from labeled training data. Each rule describes a condition on input features and a predicted class or outcome. Unlike black-box models, rules are directly human-interpretable and can be validated, overridden, and explained to domain experts.

Key Concepts

  • Antecedent: A conjunction of attribute conditions that define when the rule fires. Example: age < 30 AND income > 50000
  • Consequent: The predicted class or action. Example: THEN credit_risk = LOW
  • Coverage: The proportion of training examples that satisfy the antecedent. Low coverage = the rule applies to few cases.
  • Accuracy (Confidence): Among examples that satisfy the antecedent, the proportion that also have the predicted consequent: P(consequent | antecedent). Low confidence means the rule often fires incorrectly.
  • Support: The proportion of all training examples that satisfy both antecedent and consequent.
  • Lift: Confidence / P(consequent). Lift > 1 means the antecedent positively predicts the consequent beyond the base rate.

Types of Rules

Classification Rules

Assign a class label based on feature conditions. Produced by RIPPER (Repeated Incremental Pruning to Produce Error Reduction) and decision-tree-derived rule extraction:

IF age < 30 AND income > 50000 THEN credit_risk = LOW    (cov=32%, acc=88%)
IF age >= 60 AND debt_ratio > 0.5 THEN credit_risk = HIGH (cov=15%, acc=91%)
OTHERWISE THEN credit_risk = MEDIUM

Association Rules

Capture co-occurrence patterns in transaction data without a predefined target variable. Produced by Apriori and FP-Growth:

IF {bread, butter} THEN {milk}   (support=0.40, confidence=0.75, lift=1.80)

Interpretation: 40% of all transactions contain bread + butter + milk. Of transactions with bread and butter, 75% also have milk. This lift of 1.8 means bread+butter buyers are 1.8× more likely to buy milk than the average customer.

Ordered Rule Lists vs Unordered Rule Sets

An ordered decision list fires rules in sequence — the first rule whose antecedent matches classifies the instance; no further rules are checked. An unordered rule set allows multiple rules to fire simultaneously; conflicts are resolved by confidence weighting or voting. Decision lists are more predictable; rule sets can capture multiple overlapping patterns.

RIPPER Algorithm — Overview

RIPPER (Cohen, 1995) builds classification rules by repeatedly growing a rule to cover positive cases, pruning it against separate data to reduce overfitting, removing covered examples and repeating until a stopping criterion is reached. It then revisits rules to improve the set. A default rule handles cases not covered by earlier rules; evaluate the resulting list on held-out examples.

7. Neural Networks

Separate supervised weight updates and generalization from unsupervised competitive learning, where neurons specialize without labels.

Practice: Q10 · competitive learning · Q28 · SVM vs networks · Q40 · learning and generalization.

Definition

An artificial neural network (ANN) is a layered computational model that learns a mapping from inputs to outputs by adjusting connection weights through iterative training. With suitable nonlinear activations and sufficient capacity, some feedforward networks can approximate continuous functions on bounded domains; approximation alone does not guarantee learning or generalization.

Architecture and Components

ComponentDescriptionDetails
Input Layer Receives raw features One neuron per input feature. No transformation — values passed forward as-is.
Hidden Layers Transform features into representations Each neuron computes: output = f(w·x + b) where f is an activation function. Multiple hidden layers enable hierarchical feature learning.
Output Layer Produces final prediction Sigmoid (binary classification), Softmax (multi-class), Linear (regression).
Weights (w) Learnable parameters on connections Usually initialized and updated using gradients computed by backpropagation; parameter count depends on actual layer sizes and connections.
Biases (b) Learnable offset per neuron Allows the activation threshold to shift, equivalent to an intercept term.
Activation Function Non-linearity that enables learning complex patterns ReLU (max(0,x)), Sigmoid (1/(1+e⁻ˣ)), Tanh, Leaky ReLU, GELU, Swish.
Loss Function Quantifies prediction error MSE for regression; Binary Cross-Entropy for binary classification; Categorical Cross-Entropy for multi-class.

Backpropagation — The Learning Algorithm

Forward pass: Input is propagated layer by layer. Each neuron computes z = w·x + b, then applies activation a = f(z). The output layer produces a prediction ŷ.

Backward pass: The loss L is computed (e.g., L = −Σ yi log(ŷi) for cross-entropy). The gradient ∂L/∂w is computed for each weight using the chain rule, propagating the error backward layer by layer from output to input. Weights are updated: w ← w − α · (∂L/∂w), where α is the learning rate.

This process repeats for many mini-batches across many epochs (full passes through the training set) until the loss converges or a validation metric stops improving.

Generalization — Preventing Overfitting

Overfitting occurs when the network memorizes training examples rather than learning the underlying input-output mapping. Techniques to improve generalization:

  • Dropout: During training, randomly zero out a chosen fraction p of neuron outputs. This prevents co-adaptation and forces the network to learn redundant representations.
  • L2 Regularization (Weight Decay): Add λ||w||² to the loss. Penalizes large weights, encouraging simpler models.
  • Early Stopping: Monitor validation loss; stop training when validation loss begins increasing even though training loss is still decreasing.
  • Data Augmentation: Artificially expand the training set via random transformations (rotation, flipping, cropping for images; synonym replacement for text).
  • Batch Normalization: Normalize mini-batch activations using batch statistics and learnable scale/shift parameters. It can stabilize optimization; any regularization effect is task-dependent.

Specialized Architectures

Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)

Designed for grid-structured data (images, video). Key innovation: the convolutional layer, which applies a small learnable filter (kernel) across the entire input using shared weights. This dramatically reduces parameters vs a fully-connected layer and captures local spatial patterns (edges, textures) that can be detected across positions (translation equivariance; pooling can add some invariance). Architecture: Input → [Conv + ReLU + Pooling]× N → Flatten → Fully-Connected → Output. Pooling (max pooling, average pooling) reduces spatial dimensions, building hierarchical abstractions from local to global features. Applications: image classification (ResNet, VGG), object detection (YOLO), medical imaging.

Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) / LSTM

Designed for sequential data (time series, text, audio). RNNs maintain a hidden state ht that is updated at each time step: ht = f(W·ht−1 + U·xt + b). Vanilla RNNs suffer from the vanishing gradient problem for long sequences. LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) uses input, forget and output gates and an additive cell-state update to help retain useful information across steps; it mitigates, but does not eliminate, long-range learning difficulties.

Filled circles (output layer) = predictions. Gray lines = weighted connections (each has its own learnable weight). Competitive Learning

Competitive learning is an unsupervised learning paradigm where output neurons compete to represent each input. Only the "winning" neuron (with weights closest to the input, measured by Euclidean distance) updates its weights: wwinner = wwinner + η(x − wwinner). Over many inputs, different neurons specialize in representing different regions of the input distribution.

Worked update: For x = (2, 3), prototype weights wA = (1, 3) and wB = (5, 5), A wins because its Euclidean distance to x is 1 versus √13 for B. At learning rate η = 0.2, update A to (1, 3) + 0.2((2, 3) − (1, 3)) = (1.2, 3); B stays at (5, 5). Repeated inputs near A draw its prototype toward that region. Unlike backpropagation, this winner-take-all step requires no target labels.

Self-Organizing Map (SOM / Kohonen Network): Extends competitive learning by organizing neurons on a 2D grid and updating not just the winner but also its topological neighbors (with decreasing influence further from the winner). The neighborhood radius shrinks over training. The goal is a 2D map that approximately preserves neighborhood relationships, though topology preservation is not guaranteed. Used for: unsupervised data visualization, clustering, anomaly detection, and dimensionality reduction for qualitative interpretation.

8. Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

PCA rotates centered data onto variance-ranked orthogonal directions; compare its linear representation with learned neural-network bottlenecks.

Practice: Q11 · PCA definition · Q20 · steps and example · Q33 · PCA in a pipeline.

Definition and Purpose

PCA is an unsupervised dimensionality reduction technique that transforms a dataset with p correlated variables into k ≤ p uncorrelated linear combinations called principal components (PCs). PCs are ordered by the proportion of total variance they explain (PC1 captures the most). PCA is used to: eliminate multicollinearity, reduce storage and computation costs, enable 2D/3D visualization of high-dimensional data, and extract latent structure.

Step-by-Step Algorithm

  1. Standardize: For each feature j, compute zij = (xij − μj) / σj. This ensures features measured on different scales contribute equally. If features are on the same scale, standardization may be skipped (use the covariance matrix instead of the correlation matrix).
  2. Compute the covariance matrix C: The p×p symmetric matrix where Cij = cov(feature i, feature j). C = (1/(n−1)) × ZᵀZ where Z is the standardized data matrix.
  3. Eigendecompose C: Solve C·v = λ·v. Each eigenvector v defines a direction in feature space (a PC direction); the eigenvalue λ is the variance of the data projected onto that direction.
  4. Sort by eigenvalue (descending): PC1 corresponds to the largest eigenvalue (captures maximum variance); PC2 has the second-largest eigenvalue and is orthogonal to PC1; etc.
  5. Select top k components: Choose k from a scree plot or a cumulative explained-variance target appropriate to the task. Explained variance ratio of PCi = λi / Σλj. For standardized features, the eigenvalue-greater-than-1 (Kaiser) rule is one heuristic, not a guarantee of useful components.
  6. Project: Arrange the selected eigenvectors as columns of a p×k loading matrix Vk. Multiply the centered (and, if chosen, standardized) n×p data matrix Z by Vk to get the n×k score matrix.

Worked PCA Example and Neural-Network Connection

Take two same-scale measurements for three cases: (1,1), (2,2), (3,3). Centering gives (−1,−1), (0,0), (1,1). The covariance matrix is [1 1; 1 1], with eigenvalues 2 and 0. PC1 points along (1,1)/√2; projecting onto it gives scores −√2, 0, √2. Here one component preserves all sample variance because the two measurements duplicate one another. In real data, high variance need not be predictive of a particular label.

A linear autoencoder trained on centered data with a one-dimensional bottleneck and squared reconstruction loss can learn the same principal subspace as PCA at a suitable global optimum; its individual weights need not equal PCA's axes. Nonlinear activations allow curved representations, but no longer imply PCA. PCA and autoencoders learn from inputs without labels; a supervised neural classifier instead optimizes prediction against labels.

Scree Plot and Component Selection

For seven standardized variables, the seven illustrated eigenvalues sum to 7; the drop followed by smaller changes around PC3–PC4 suggests testing 3 components. The dashed horizontal line at eigenvalue = 1 is the Kaiser criterion — for standardized features, PCs above this line (PC1–PC3 here) exceed the variance of one standardized original variable; selection still depends on the task.

PCA Limitations

  • PCA captures only linear relationships — it cannot discover nonlinear structure (use Kernel PCA or t-SNE/UMAP for nonlinear dimensionality reduction).
  • PCA components are linear combinations of all original features — they may lack direct interpretability (unlike the original variables).
  • PCA is sensitive to outliers — a single extreme outlier can distort the direction of the first principal component. Robust PCA methods (PCP) address this.
  • Choose centering and scaling deliberately: without standardization, variables with larger numerical variance can dominate PCA even if their units are arbitrary; standardization is not always desirable when scales are already meaningful.

9. Fuzzy Logic

Fuzzy membership expresses graded category fit; learn how to derive membership functions and interpretable rules or trees from observations.

Practice: Q8 · fuzzy-logic short note · Q30 · compare crisp rule induction.

Definition

Fuzzy logic (Lotfi Zadeh, 1965) extends classical binary logic by allowing truth values in the continuous range [0, 1] rather than only {0, 1}. It mathematically models vague, linguistic concepts — "hot," "fast," "tall," "slightly above average" — using membership functions that assign a degree of membership between 0 (completely outside the set) and 1 (fully inside the set). Fuzzy logic reasons about graded categories; a membership degree is not automatically a probability of an event.

Crisp Sets vs Fuzzy Sets

In a crisp (classical) set, an element either belongs to the set (membership = 1) or does not (membership = 0). A person 179 cm tall: NOT tall (if the threshold is 180 cm). In a fuzzy set, the same person has membership 0.8 in the "tall" set — "mostly tall." This gradual membership is more faithful to human perception and natural language.

Four Steps of a Fuzzy Logic System

  1. Fuzzification: Convert a crisp input value into fuzzy membership degrees across all relevant fuzzy sets. Example using Figure 8's membership functions: at 26°C, membership in "warm" is about 0.67, while "cold" and "hot" are both 0.
  2. Rule Evaluation: Apply IF-THEN linguistic rules using fuzzy operators. AND = min(memberships), OR = max(memberships), NOT = 1 − membership. Example: IF temperature IS warm AND humidity IS high THEN fan_speed IS medium.
  3. Aggregation: Combine the fuzzy output sets from all rules that fired into a single combined output fuzzy set.
  4. Defuzzification: Convert the combined fuzzy output set back to a single crisp value. One common method is the centroid (center of area of the output fuzzy set), which can produce a smoothly varying crisp output.

Extracting Fuzzy Models from Data

Instead of hand-writing every linguistic rule, use measured input–output pairs to learn a fuzzy model. For temperature and observed fan speed: (1) inspect the input range and define overlapping terms such as Cool, Warm and Hot; (2) initialize membership shapes from domain knowledge or clusters of observations; (3) form candidate IF–THEN rules, such as IF temperature is Hot THEN fan speed is High, and estimate output values from matching cases; (4) tune membership parameters and rule outputs to reduce prediction error; (5) simplify redundant rules and test on held-out readings. A fitted rule can describe an association in these data without establishing a causal mechanism.

Fuzzy Decision Trees

A crisp decision tree sends an input down exactly one branch at each split. A fuzzy tree can send it down multiple branches with different membership strengths: a reading of 32°C in Figure 8 has Warm membership (34 − 32)/12 ≈ 0.17 and Hot membership (32 − 28)/10 = 0.40. Each branch evaluates its consequent (for example, Medium or High fan speed); a model-specific weighting and normalization scheme combines leaf outputs. The memberships need not sum to 1 before normalization. Fuzzy splits soften abrupt threshold changes, but too many overlapping rules can make the tree hard to interpret or overfit.

ANFIS — Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System

ANFIS (Jang, 1993) combines fuzzy logic reasoning with neural network learning. It is a 5-layer network: Layer 1 computes membership degrees for each input (fuzzification); Layer 2 computes rule firing strengths (product of memberships); Layer 3 normalizes firing strengths; Layer 4 computes weighted consequents; Layer 5 combines weighted rule consequents into a crisp output (a Sugeno-style weighted result). Membership and consequent parameters can be learned from data by gradient-based and hybrid optimization; learned rules still require validation and may not always be easy to interpret.

Applications

  • Control systems: Anti-lock braking (ABS), elevator scheduling, camera autofocus, industrial process control (cement kilns, blast furnaces)
  • Decision support: Medical diagnosis (patient condition as "slightly elevated risk"), credit risk assessment, investment rating
  • Data analysis: Fuzzy clustering (Fuzzy C-Means) for soft cluster assignment, where a data point can belong partially to multiple clusters

10. Stochastic Search and Optimization

When exhaustive or gradient-based search is impractical, use random proposals and an objective function to explore candidate solutions.

Practice: Q15 · definition and application · Q33 · choose methods for a dataset.

Definition

Stochastic search is a class of metaheuristic optimization algorithms that use controlled randomness to explore complex, high-dimensional search spaces — particularly when the objective function is non-differentiable, multi-modal (many local optima), or so complex that gradient-based methods get trapped in local optima. Stochastic search sacrifices the guarantee of finding the global optimum for practical tractability in large search spaces.

The No Free Lunch Theorem

Wolpert and Macready (1997) proved that no optimization algorithm performs better than random search on average across all possible objective functions. This means no stochastic search algorithm is universally superior — the best choice depends on the structure of the specific problem. Understanding this theorem motivates empirical comparison of multiple algorithms on each new problem.

Genetic Algorithms (GA)

GAs (Holland, 1975) are evolutionary algorithms inspired by natural selection and genetics. A population of candidate solutions (individuals) evolves over generations through three operators:

  1. Selection: Evaluate each individual's fitness (objective function value). Select parents with probability proportional to fitness (better solutions are more likely to reproduce). Common methods: roulette wheel selection, tournament selection.
  2. Crossover (Recombination): Combine two parent solutions to produce offspring. Single-point crossover: pick a random cut point; offspring 1 takes parent 1's genes before the cut and parent 2's genes after; vice versa for offspring 2.
  3. Mutation: Randomly alter genes in the offspring with a chosen mutation probability. This maintains diversity and prevents premature convergence to local optima.

The algorithm iterates until a termination criterion is met (maximum generations, fitness threshold, no improvement for k generations).

GA Example — Binary Feature Selection

Problem: 10 features available; search for a subset with good validation performance. Search space: 2¹⁰ = 1,024 possible subsets. Encode each candidate as a 10-bit string (1 = included, 0 = excluded); score it using cross-validation on the training portion, optionally penalizing large subsets. Selection, crossover and mutation test new candidates. Keep a separate final test set: stochastic search does not guarantee the best subset and must not tune on test data.

Simulated Annealing (SA)

SA (Kirkpatrick, 1983) is inspired by the metallurgical annealing process — slowly cooling a material to minimize its energy. The algorithm:

  1. Start with an initial solution s and temperature T0 (high).
  2. Propose a small random modification s' of the current solution.
  3. If f(s') < f(s) (improvement): accept s' unconditionally.
  4. If f(s') ≥ f(s) (worsening): accept s' with probability exp(−Δf / T), where Δf = f(s') − f(s). At high T, almost any worsening move is accepted (exploration). As T decreases, fewer worsening moves are accepted (exploitation).
  5. Reduce T according to a cooling schedule (geometric: Tk+1 = α × Tk, with 0 < α < 1).
  6. Repeat until T → 0 or a stopping criterion is reached.

SA is proved to converge to the global optimum given a sufficiently slow cooling schedule (though this may require exponential time). Practical finite schedules trade computation for solution quality without a near-optimality guarantee.

Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)

PSO (Kennedy & Eberhart, 1995) simulates a swarm of particles flying through the search space. Each particle i has a position xi (candidate solution), velocity vi, and remembers its personal best position pi. The swarm collectively tracks the global best position g. At each iteration: vi ← w·vi + c1·r1·(pi − xi) + c2·r2·(g − xi); xi ← xi + vi. The inertia weight w balances exploration (high w) vs exploitation (low w). PSO is often used for continuous optimization, but its quality depends on the objective and parameter settings.

Application in Data Analysis

  • Hyperparameter tuning: Finding the optimal learning rate, number of trees, regularization strength for an ML model — solved by Bayesian Optimization or Random Search over the hyperparameter space.
  • Feature selection: Genetic algorithms select the best subset of features for a predictive model without exhaustive enumeration.
  • Neural network architecture search (NAS): Evolutionary algorithms search the space of neural network architectures (number of layers, layer widths, connection patterns) to find architectures that maximize accuracy on a target task.
  • Clustering: K-means uses random initialization of centroids; multiple restarts with different initializations avoid poor local optima.
